Orbit — the mark, third pass

Both wordmark passes so far — concept B's small ring beside the word and B′'s whole-word sweep, whose ring only ever ran behind on its far arc and in front on its near one — were turned down. This round works from the owner's own sketch instead: a large, noticeably tilted ring threaded through the word, weaving in front of some strokes and genuinely behind others, with a small world riding the ring near its upper crossing. Part 1 pushes that one idea through four finishes. Part 2 sets it aside and tries five different ways the mark could carry the identity instead.

Part 1 — B³ · the threaded ring

One geometry, hand-tuned once and reused by every finish below: a wide ellipse tilted ~28°, pulled left and up from the word's centre so its true apex — the ring's actual topmost point — sits clear in the air above and right of the t. From the apex the ring sweeps down and left: the moment its height crosses the word's tallest ascender, paint order flips to behind; the moment it drops back past the baseline, it flips to front again for the long way home under the word and back up to the apex. Nothing about that flip is per-letter — it's one continuous dive past real ink, so whichever strokes happen to sit in the way (here: the t, the i, and the b) genuinely occlude the ring where their ink is, and let it show through in the gaps between.

Split points, not eyeballed per-mark: every card above shares one buildWeaveMark() pass. It measures the rendered word's box and, for the letters most likely to carry an ascender, the actual ink top via canvas metrics — not a guessed pixel value. From those it builds one ellipse (tilt, radius factors and a centre offset are the hand-tuned constants), samples it to find the true apex, then walks forward from the apex until the curve's height crosses the tallest ascender — that's where paint order flips to behind — and walks on until it crosses the baseline, where it flips back to front. Everywhere else the ring is front by default, since there's no ink up there to hide it anyway. The only numbers nudged by eye were the ellipse's tilt, size and centre — enough attempts to get that one dive to land squarely on the ascenders instead of floating clear of the word.
